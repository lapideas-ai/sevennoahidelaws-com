# SHEM AND EBER — SOURCE DISCIPLINE

Jewish tradition associates Shem, the son of Noah, and Eber, his descendant, with an ancient center or school of spiritual learning that existed before Sinai.

Traditional sources associate Abraham, Isaac, Jacob and the patriarchal world with this earlier tradition.

However, Claude must NEVER turn later Jewish tradition into the plain meaning of a biblical verse without identifying the distinction.

Use the following hierarchy:

1. **What Genesis explicitly says**
2. **What ancient/rabbinic Jewish tradition says**
3. **What later commentators say**
4. **Where commentators disagree**
5. **What 7NL proposes as interpretation or a question worth considering**

This distinction is essential to 7NL's credibility.

We want readers to explore extraordinary ideas without ever misleading them about the source of those ideas.

# REBECCA'S QUESTION — A MODEL FOR 7NL CONTENT

Genesis 25:22 describes Rebecca experiencing an extraordinary struggle during her pregnancy and says:

**She "went to inquire of the LORD."**

The biblical verse itself does NOT identify whom she consulted.

Traditional Jewish interpretation adds important possibilities.

Rashi, drawing upon Genesis Rabbah, says Rebecca went to the school of Shem to inquire of G-d.

Rashi further explains that the answer was revealed to Shem and communicated to Rebecca.

Other commentators preserve alternative possibilities.

Ibn Ezra allows that the divine answer may have come through a prophet or even Abraham himself.

Radak draws attention to the striking question of why Rebecca, according to the Shem tradition, would bypass her father-in-law Abraham even though Abraham was still alive.

Therefore NEVER write:

**"The Bible says Rebecca went to Shem."**

Instead write:

**"Genesis says Rebecca went to inquire of G-d. An ancient Jewish tradition preserved by Genesis Rabbah and Rashi says she went to the school of Shem."**

That distinction exemplifies the editorial integrity 7NL should maintain everywhere.

# THE PRE-ABRAHAMIC UNIVERSAL TRADITION

7NL should resist telling biblical history as though humanity's relationship
with G-d begins with Abraham, Israel, Sinai, or Judaism.

The biblical story begins with humanity.

A more useful progression is:

**Adam → Noah → Noahic Covenant → Shem/Eber Tradition → Abraham →
Melchizedek → Abrahamic Covenant → Isaac/Jacob → Israel → Sinai**

This chronology matters.

Genesis explicitly describes a covenant between G-d, Noah, and ultimately
"all flesh."

Later, G-d establishes a distinct covenant with Abraham and his descendants.

Still later comes Sinai and the covenantal obligations associated with Israel.

Therefore, avoid language suggesting that Sinai introduced the first
relationship, covenant, or moral obligation between G-d and humanity.

A central 7NL question is:

**What belonged to humanity before there was a Jewish nation?**

# MELCHIZEDEK — AN IMPORTANT BRIDGE

The story of Abraham itself contains evidence that knowledge and service of
G-d existed outside Abraham's own family.

After Abram goes to war to rescue Lot, Genesis 14 introduces:

**Melchizedek, King of Salem**

whom Genesis explicitly calls:

**"priest of God Most High."**

Melchizedek blesses Abram in the name of God Most High, Creator of heaven
and earth, and Abram gives him a tenth.

This episode deserves substantial attention within 7NL.

It demonstrates from Genesis itself — without depending upon later
commentary — that Abraham encounters another recognized servant of G-d
outside his immediate family.

Do not overstate what Genesis establishes about Melchizedek.

Later Jewish and other religious traditions identify and interpret
Melchizedek in different ways. Those traditions should be clearly labeled
when discussed.

But Genesis 14 itself provides an important beginner question:

**If Abraham was the only person who knew G-d, who was Melchizedek?**

That question opens the door to exploring the older universal tradition
that precedes Israel.

# REBECCA — INQUIRY RATHER THAN "BYPASSING"

Never state that Rebecca "bypassed Abraham."

Genesis 25 says that during the painful and extraordinary struggle within
her pregnancy, Rebecca:

**"went to inquire of the LORD."**

Genesis does not tell us everything she had already discussed with Abraham,
Isaac, Sarah, family members, or others.

Traditional Jewish sources identify Shem or the academy associated with
Shem as part of this episode.

The interesting question is therefore NOT:

**Why did Rebecca reject Abraham?**

Instead ask:

**What could the older tradition represented by Shem offer Rebecca when
ordinary family wisdom or comfort may not have been enough for what she
was experiencing?**

This is a question, not an established answer.

The divine response itself dramatically enlarges the issue:

**"Two nations are in your womb."**

Rebecca's suffering was connected to something larger than an ordinary
pregnancy or even one family.

This makes it especially interesting to explore why Jewish tradition
connects her inquiry with Shem — a figure connected through Noah to the
universal human story.

Again:

**Genesis tells us what happened.**

**Tradition adds another layer.**

**7NL can then ask what that layer might mean.**

Never collapse those three categories into one.

# THREE GREAT COVENANTAL STAGES

For introductory purposes, 7NL should teach the progression carefully:

## 1. NOAH

After the Flood, Genesis describes G-d establishing a covenant involving
Noah, his descendants, living creatures, and the earth.

Its sign is the rainbow.

This is explicitly universal in scope.

## 2. ABRAHAM

Genesis later describes G-d establishing an everlasting covenant with
Abraham and his descendants.

Abraham is promised descendants, nations, kings, and a particular
covenantal future.

Circumcision becomes a sign of this covenant.

## 3. SINAI

The biblical story later reaches Sinai and Israel's national covenantal
life and commandments.

Sinai should therefore be understood within an existing history of
covenants rather than as the beginning of humanity's relationship with G-d.

The pedagogical progression is:

**Humanity**

→ **Noah**

→ **Universal Covenant**

→ **Abraham**

→ **Abrahamic Covenant**

→ **Israel**

→ **Sinai**

This distinction is central to explaining why someone does not have to
become Jewish before asking:

**What does G-d expect of me as a human being?**

# MELCHIZEDEK — THE BLESSING AND TRANSFER OF PRIESTHOOD

The Melchizedek episode contains an important later Jewish interpretation
that must be presented with proper source attribution.

Genesis 14:18–20 describes Melchizedek as:

**"priest of God Most High."**

He blesses Abram first and afterward blesses G-d Most High.

The Babylonian Talmud, Nedarim 32b, specifically notices this ordering.

According to the teaching attributed there to Rabbi Zekharya in the name
of Rabbi Yishmael, G-d originally intended the priesthood to emerge from
Shem, whom this tradition identifies with Melchizedek.

But Melchizedek placed the blessing of Abraham before the blessing of G-d.

The Talmud depicts Abraham challenging this order:

Why should the servant be blessed before his Master?

According to this rabbinic interpretation, the priesthood consequently
passed from the descendants of Shem to the descendants of Abraham.

EDITORIAL DISCIPLINE:

Do NOT say:

"Genesis says Melchizedek lost the priesthood."

Genesis does not say this.

Instead say:

"Genesis records Melchizedek blessing Abram before blessing G-d.
The Talmud later interprets this ordering as consequential and teaches
that the priesthood passed from Shem's line to Abraham's."

This is another excellent example of the 7NL method:

**Bible → Tradition → Interpretation → Question**

It also adds another dimension to the transition:

**Noah → Shem/Melchizedek → Abraham**

The transition should be explored rather than reduced to a simplistic
claim that Abraham suddenly introduced knowledge of G-d into a world in
which no such tradition existed.
